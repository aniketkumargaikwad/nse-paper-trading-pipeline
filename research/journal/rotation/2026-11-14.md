# Rotation research notes — 2026-11-14

## 12-0 momentum, 30 stocks, NIFTY 200-day filter (breadth robustness test)

**Training outcome:** -1.58%/yr vs the paper rule (t -1.19), worst fall 22.3%, beat NIFTY in 88% of years - fails: no better than the momentum rule already paper-traded (-1.58% a year against it)

**Lessons:** Holding count is not where the edge lies. More names give marginally lower drawdown but cost about 1.6% a year, and the hit is largest in the best momentum years. Going the other way (fewer names) is mostly a hindsight-prone concentration bet. Further tuning of position count is unlikely to beat the paper-traded baseline. A real improvement needs a different signal or risk rule, not a different breadth of the same signal.

## 12-0 momentum, 25 stocks, NIFTY 200-day filter (breadth monotonicity check)

**Training outcome:** +0.68%/yr vs the paper rule (t 0.36), worst fall 22.7%, beat NIFTY in 82% of years - fails: its lead over the paper-traded rule could be luck: t = 0.36 (needs 1.65)

**Lessons:** Portfolio size between 20 and 25 is a flat parameter: return and drawdown are essentially unchanged, so it is not a lever for beating the baseline. Small tweaks to breadth of the same 12-1 ranking mostly reshuffle the marginal names and produce year-to-year noise, not an edge. To get t > 1.65 versus the baseline, the change needs to alter which stocks get picked or when the strategy is exposed, not just how many.

## 12-1 momentum, 15 stocks, NIFTY 200-day filter (driver isolation)

**Training outcome:** +1.57%/yr vs the paper rule (t 1.43), worst fall 25.4%, beat NIFTY in 76% of years - fails: its lead over the paper-traded rule could be luck: t = 1.43 (needs 1.65)

**Lessons:** Changing how many stocks the rule holds mostly trades risk for return. It cannot produce a lead over the baseline that passes the luck test. Gains that sit in two or three momentum boom years should not be trusted. Future ideas should change the signal or the risk control, for example weighting by volatility or a different lookback, and still beat the baseline in more than half the years. The hindsight bias in the absolute numbers also argues against chasing small CAGR gains.
